## 中文原文（verbatim）

来聊一个大家做美研项目研究和录取偏好分析时，很容易忽略的地方：官网里的学生分享。今天就以 MIT 商业分析硕士 MBAn 官网的 Meet the Class 为例，看看问题框架本身如何作为我们研究项目的起点，以及梳理文书的 guideline。

首先，从在读学生为什么选择这个项目，看他们背后的真实需求，然后思考你会怎么回答这个问题。这个可以直接和文书里的 why program 连接。很多人写这个板块，会从课程、capstone 和就业资源开始堆积。但重点是这些资源之前的认知铺垫：你已经具备什么能力，又在哪一步发现了不足，为什么这些资源对你重要？比如 Annie 提到，她有数学和计量经济学基础，但希望进一步学会应用，因此看重项目的技术深度和企业实践。这里值得参考的是"已有基础—能力缺口—项目资源"，是一个很好运用到自己文书里的逻辑。从自己需求出发的 why program，是最有个人特色的。

其次，他们分享喜欢的课程中，核心都是看这门课真正帮助自己解决了什么问题。所以你在文书中列举的课程，一定是你真正感兴趣或者需要的，而不是为了显得专业，选择那些难度和技能比较高的课程。例如，在优化课程中，用所学方法研究卡坦游戏的初始布局和逻辑。一个看起来很日常的兴趣，也可以成为认真分析的问题和动机。

第三，从他们参与的社团和活动，看自己还能做出什么实际贡献，核心就是体现 diversity。不仅是强调背景不同，而是在这些兴趣，经验和思考方式的差异中如何建立一个共同成长的 community。那如果你思考贡献，是做重复资源的提升，还是能带来新的内容和参与机会？我们需要回到自己的经历中找支撑点。比如，你过去激励不同背景的人参与讨论，并且主动分享自己的经验，解决了团队合作问题。这些都可以成为素材，然后在进一步分享你如何把这些经验带入这个 community。

最后，从他们给申请者的建议，看自己目前的申请定位。基本都指向三个方面：清晰的自我认知，如何贡献和帮助 community，快速建立 connection 和资源整合。项目强调解决真实问题，那你的简历是否只罗列了工具和模型，却没有解释解决了什么和如何应用？如果强调个人贡献，你的文书是否只写了想获得什么，却没有体现你对于行业的思考和清晰的 next steps。

## English Reading Guide

Official program websites publish "Meet the Class" / student profile pages, and the questions the program chooses to ask its students are a direct window into admissions priorities. Instead of treating these pages as marketing fluff, use the question framework itself as a research instrument and essay specification. MIT MBAn example: every profile asks why they chose the program, favorite course, clubs/activities, and advice to applicants — each maps to a distinct essay dimension.

### The four-question → four-essay-dimension mapping

**1. "Why did you choose this program?" → Why Program (fit logic, not resource listing)**

The common mistake: dumping course names + capstone + career services. The correct logic chain is:

- **Existing foundation** — what you already bring (skills, knowledge, experience)
- **Capability gap** — the specific moment/point where you discovered a deficiency
- **Program resources** — why THESE resources close THAT gap for YOU

Annie example: math + econometrics background → wanted to learn *application* → valued the program's technical depth + corporate practice. The resources only matter because of the gap that precedes them. A why-program essay written from your own need is inherently distinctive; resource lists are interchangeable across applicants.

**2. "Favorite course?" → Course selection must show problem-driven interest, not signaling**

Students talk about what the course let them *solve*, not how hard it was. Essay implication: name courses you genuinely need, and demonstrate genuine interest by attaching a concrete question or curiosity — e.g., using optimization methods to analyze initial settlement placement logic in the board game Catan. An everyday interest treated as a serious analytical question is stronger than listing the hardest course to look technical.

**3. "Clubs and activities?" → Diversity contribution = additive, not demographic**

Diversity is not just claiming a different background. It is showing how differences in interests, experience, and thinking style build a community that grows together. Test your contribution claim: are you merely improving an existing/duplicative resource, or bringing new content and participation opportunities? Mine your own history for evidence — e.g., you previously motivated people from different backgrounds to join discussions, proactively shared your experience, and solved a team-collaboration problem — then project that exact behavior into the target community.

**4. "Advice to applicants?" → Self-positioning audit**

Their advice converges on three things: clear self-awareness, how to contribute to community, rapid connection-building and resource integration. Audit your own materials against the program's emphasis on solving real problems:

- **Resume check:** do your bullets only list tools and models without explaining what problem you solved and how you applied them? (Fix: problem → approach → impact)
- **Essay check:** do you only write about what you want to *get*, without showing industry thinking and clear next steps? (Fix: contribution + post-program trajectory)

## English Key Takeaways

1. **The question list is the rubric.** Whatever questions a program publicly asks its current students (why here / favorite class / clubs / advice) are the dimensions admissions weighs — build your research spreadsheet from those question columns.
2. **Why Program = foundation → gap → resource.** Never lead with resources; establish what you have, where you hit a wall, and only then why specific program elements close that gap. Generic resource stacking signals low self-awareness.
3. **Courses need a question attached.** Choose courses you actually need; prove interest by naming a concrete problem or even a playful-but-serious analytical question (Catan + optimization).
4. **Diversity means additive contribution.** Bring something new — content, participation, bridging people across backgrounds — not just a demographic label. Evidence comes from past behavior: when did you draw different people in and make the group work better?
5. **Resume bullets must state problem + application + outcome**, not a tool/model inventory.
6. **Essays need next steps and industry thinking**, not only "what I will gain." Admissions wants trajectory and contribution, not consumption intent.
7. **Operational method:** for every target program, read 8–15 student profiles, tabulate answers per question, extract recurring patterns (that's the program's real preference), then reverse-engineer your own answers using the same four frames.
